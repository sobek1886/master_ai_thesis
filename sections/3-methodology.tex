This chapter presents the methodological contribution of the thesis: a
formal framing of signer appearance as an intervenable nuisance factor
(\S\ref{sec:method-notation}), a taxonomy of appearance counterfactuals
together with two synthesis routes that realise them
(\S\ref{sec:method-diffusion}), and training objectives that consume the
counterfactuals either as additional data or as explicit positives
(\S\ref{sec:method-objectives}). The concrete instantiation (datasets,
backbones, training configurations, and evaluation protocols) is
collected in \S\ref{sec:setup-datasets}--\ref{sec:eval-protocols}.

\section{Problem formulation}
\label{sec:method-notation}
% Formal notation block (2026-08-03): set/space definitions + typed maps in
% the register of Nalmpantis §4.1-4.2 (run-in headers, one object per
% paragraph, numbered display equations), grounded in this thesis's own task.
This section fixes the notation used throughout the chapter: the data we
train on, the encoder under study, the appearance-counterfactual operator
that both synthesis routes instantiate, and the retrieval task on which
every model is evaluated.

\paragraph{Videos and labels.}
Let $\mathcal{X}$ denote the space of signing videos. A training corpus is
a finite set of annotated clips
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dataset}
\mathcal{D} \;=\; \bigl\{(x_i,\, y_i,\, s_i)\bigr\}_{i=1}^{n}
\;\subset\; \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y} \times \mathcal{S},
\end{equation}
where $y_i \in \mathcal{Y}$ is the linguistic label of the video $x_i$ and
$s_i \in \mathcal{S}$ identifies its signer; when convenient we write
$y(x)$ and $s(x)$ for the label and signer of a clip. The label space
$\mathcal{Y}$ differs between our two supervision regimes. On the isolated,
gloss-labelled benchmarks each video clip shows a single sign and
$\mathcal{Y} = \mathcal{G}$, a gloss vocabulary of $C = |\mathcal{G}|$
classes shared across signers. The continuous corpus supplies no gloss
vocabulary: full sentences are the unit of recording, and $\mathcal{Y}$ is
the set of recorded sentence identities. Throughout, the signer is a
nuisance variable: labels in $\mathcal{Y}$ are defined by what is signed,
never by who signs it, and the retrieval tasks of this thesis ask the
representation to match clips across $\mathcal{S}$ at fixed
$\mathcal{Y}$-label.

\paragraph{Encoder.}
Every training setting, whatever its objective, produces a single video
encoder
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:encoder}
f_\theta : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^{d},
\qquad z \;=\; f_\theta(x),
\end{equation}
mapping a video clip to one embedding vector, where $\theta$ collects the
trainable parameters: a video--text head over frozen backbone features,
the fine-tuned backbone itself, or the view-contrastive encoder; the
instantiations, and the dimension $d$ each uses, are given in
\S\ref{sec:setup-training}. Whether $z$ is $\ell_2$-normalised is a
property of the objective and is stated with each loss. After training,
$\theta$ is frozen and every analysis in this thesis (retrieval,
probing, feature geometry) operates on the embeddings $z$ alone.

\paragraph{Appearance counterfactual operator.}
The interventions of this thesis are appearance edits: maps
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cf-operator}
T_a : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{X}, \qquad a \in \mathcal{A},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}$ indexes the available edit types and $T_a(x)$ shows
the signing of $x$ under a changed appearance. The defining intent is that
$T_a$ moves a video clip across the nuisance factor while leaving the linguistic
content intact,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:motion-preservation}
y\bigl(T_a(x)\bigr) \;=\; y(x) \qquad \text{for all } a \in \mathcal{A},
\end{equation}
while the appearance (and, for identity-changing edits, the apparent
signer) differs. Whether Eq.~\eqref{eq:motion-preservation} actually
holds is a property of the synthesis route, not an assumption we grant
ourselves: the mocap-driven avatar renders satisfy it by construction,
because every render is driven by the same captured motion, whereas the
frame-wise generative edits satisfy it only insofar as the editor leaves the
motion untouched, an empirical matter we return to when interpreting
results. For the generative-edit route, $\mathcal{A}$ is
the four-type edit taxonomy $\{\text{shirt},\, \text{glasses},\,
\text{skin tone},\, \text{signer swap}\}$; for the avatar route,
$\mathcal{A}$ ranges over avatar--viewpoint pairs
(\S\ref{sec:setup-generation}).

\paragraph{Positive sets.}
For a training video clip $x$ we write
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:cf-set}
\mathcal{T}(x) \;=\; \{x\} \,\cup\,
\bigl\{\, T_a(x) \;:\; a \in \mathcal{A}(x) \,\bigr\}
\end{equation}
for its counterfactual set, where $\mathcal{A}(x) \subseteq \mathcal{A}$
are the edits realised for $x$ (not every edit is generated for every
clip). The contrastive objectives of \S\ref{sec:method-losses} are built
on positive sets over a batch: for an anchor $u$ in a batch indexed by
$\mathcal{B}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:positive-set}
P(u) \;=\; \bigl\{\, p \in \mathcal{B} \setminus \{u\}
\;:\; p \sim u \,\bigr\},
\end{equation}
where $\sim$ is the positive-defining equivalence of the run: membership
of one counterfactual set $\mathcal{T}(x)$ (an original and its edits;
counterfactual-contrastive), a shared gloss in $\mathcal{G}$ (gloss-level
contrast across signers), or a shared recording identity (the view-contrastive setting,
where all $K$ views of one recording, real cameras and synthetic
renders alike, are positives). Everything in the batch outside $P(u)$
acts as a negative.

\paragraph{Cross-signer retrieval.}
The task is retrieval with the frozen encoder. An evaluation protocol
supplies a query set $\mathcal{Q}$ and a reference gallery $\mathcal{R}$,
each element carrying a label in $\mathcal{Y}$; for a query $q$ the
gallery is ranked by decreasing cosine similarity
$\cos\bigl(f_\theta(q),\, f_\theta(r)\bigr)$, and retrieval at rank $k$
succeeds if a correctly labelled reference appears among the $k$
highest-ranked items:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:recall-at-k}
\mathrm{R}@k \;=\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}|} \sum_{q \in \mathcal{Q}}
\mathbf{1}\Bigl[\, y(q) \in \bigl\{\, y(r) : r \in \mathcal{R}_k(q)
\bigr\} \Bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{R}_k(q) \subseteq \mathcal{R}$ are the $k$ gallery items
most similar to $q$. The protocols of \S\ref{sec:eval-protocols}
instantiate $\mathcal{Q}$ and $\mathcal{R}$ per corpus, including a
video-to-text variant in which the gallery holds text prototypes rather
than videos, and share one structural property: evaluation signers are
unseen during training, and in the continuous-corpus protocol query and
gallery are
additionally produced by disjoint signers, making the retrieval itself
cross-signer.

\section{Synthetic appearance counterfactuals}
\label{sec:method-diffusion}

\subsection{Motion-locked re-renders}
The first route is motion-capture-driven: the captured 3D motion of a real
signer is retargeted onto synthetic avatars and re-rendered under multiple
camera viewpoints (\S\ref{sec:setup-generation}). Because it starts from
ground-truth motion rather than a 2D image edit, the signing motion is
preserved by construction (the renders are \emph{motion-locked}), and it
varies camera viewpoint as well as appearance, an axis a 2D edit cannot
reach from a single input view. It requires motion-capture recordings, so
it applies only where capture is controlled.

\subsection{Generative appearance editing}
The second route edits real training videos frame-by-frame with an open
generative image editor. It needs nothing beyond the RGB video, so it
applies to any corpus. The model, compute and edited-subset statistics are
given in \S\ref{sec:setup-generation}.
Rather than
augmenting a uniform sample, targets are chosen worst-first
at the class level: every gloss is ranked by its number of distinct training
signers (ascending), with ties broken by a difficulty proxy (per-class
nearest-centroid gloss accuracy computed on the from-scratch contrastive
baseline's test embeddings, a clustering measure that degrades precisely when
embeddings organise by signer rather than by sign), and entire classes are
taken in rank order until the budget fills.
We propose a taxonomy of four independent appearance edit types
(Fig.~\ref{fig:aug_examples}); for each
selected source video we generate up to four edits, applied frame-by-frame:
\emph{shirt} (a clothing swap, the lowest-risk edit: the video backbone's own training
augmentations already include colour jitter, so this edit's marginal value
over standard colour augmentation is itself an open question), \emph{glasses}
(addition/removal, with some risk of altering facial expression near the
eyes), \emph{skin tone} (higher-risk edit for hallucination, e.g.\
occasional extra or malformed hand shapes), and \emph{signer swap} (a full
identity change, carrying the highest potential benefit for a
signer-independent task alongside the highest hallucination risk).
The skin-tone edit follows the motivation of the Monk Skin Tone scale
for inclusive coverage of apparent skin
tones~\cite{heldrethWhichSkinTone2024,
schumannConsensusSubjectivitySkin2023}.

\paragraph{Edit vocabularies.}
Three of the four edit types are parametric. \emph{Shirt} recolours the
garment to one of ten saturated colours; \emph{skin tone} targets one of
six Monk-scale tiers ($5$--$10$, the light end excluded because lighter
tones are already over-represented among the corpus signers); and
\emph{signer swap} substitutes one of ten target identities, each
specified in detail over age, gender presentation, ethnicity, hair,
eyewear and clothing, since underspecified identities drift more between
independently edited frames. \emph{Glasses} is a single fixed edit. Each
video is assigned one shirt colour, one skin-tone tier and one identity. That target is then applied to every frame
of the clip.

\paragraph{Prompt design.}
Every prompt pairs the requested change with a clause enumerating what
must not change (the shape and position of every finger and hand, wrist
angles, arm trajectories, facial expression, gaze, mouth shape, posture
and background) and a clause forbidding the model to add, duplicate or
complete any hand or arm. The latter addresses an observed failure mode:
the editor ``completes'' a natural standing pose, hallucinating raised
hands in frames where the hands are lowered or out of frame, and so
silently alters the articulators that carry the label. The skin-tone and
signer-swap prompts relax the preservation clause only where the edit
requires it. Full texts are given in Appendix~\ref{app:flux_prompts}.

\paragraph{Frame conditioning.}
Frames are edited independently: no frame is conditioned on its
predecessor or on any previously edited frame. Cross-frame coherence
therefore rests only on the fixed per-video prompt, which is what produces
the residual flicker reported in \S\ref{sec:limitations}; the compensating
property is that a bad edit cannot propagate along the clip. Two anchoring
schemes were implemented and piloted but are not used here, a frame-0
reference image and a per-identity portrait bank, because both stabilise
appearance at the cost of keeping facial expression fixed.

\section{Counterfactual-aware training objectives}
\label{sec:method-objectives}
We train encoders with these counterfactuals in three settings:
two on an isolated, gloss-labelled benchmark and one on a continuous
corpus without a gloss vocabulary; their concrete configurations
(datasets, backbones, dimensions) are given in
\S\ref{sec:setup-training}. In every setting the counterfactuals enter
the objective either as additional labelled data or as explicit
positives matched to their source video; the loss formulations below
cover both mechanisms.

\subsection{Loss formulations}
\label{sec:method-losses}
Three distinct model components are trained in this thesis, and each has
its own objective; Table~\ref{tab:objective_map} maps components to
objectives before the formulations are given. The \emph{video--text
head} trains only its own parameters on top of frozen backbone features;
the backbone receives no gradient. The \emph{backbone fine-tuning}
runs do the opposite: they update the video backbone itself, with a
disposable linear gloss head on top. The \emph{view-contrastive encoder}
of the continuous corpus again trains only its own parameters over
frozen backbone features, and has no text tower at all.

\subsubsection{Video--text head}
The video--text head is trained with the symmetric InfoNCE
loss~\cite{oordRepresentationLearningContrastive2018}
we take from VideoCLIP~\cite{xuVideoCLIPContrastivePretraining2021}, over frozen
768-d backbone features; only the head's parameters receive gradients.
For a batch of paired video and text
embeddings $(v_i, t_i)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:nce}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{NCE}} \;=\;
-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl[&
\log \frac{\exp(v_i \cdot t_i)}{\sum_{j}\exp(v_i \cdot t_j)}\\
&+ \log \frac{\exp(v_i \cdot t_i)}{\sum_{j}\exp(v_j \cdot t_i)}
\Bigr],
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where the text side is the gloss rendered as a caption; following
VideoCLIP, the embeddings are not $\ell_2$-normalised and no temperature is
applied. Synthetic counterfactuals enter in one of two ways: as additional
$(v, t)$ pairs carrying their source video's gloss (aug-as-data), or, in
the pair-SupCon configurations, by replacing the video--text loss with
the supervised-contrastive form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:supcon} below, computed
over video embeddings alone with an original and its edits sharing one
instance label (counterfactual-contrastive). 

\subsubsection{Backbone fine-tuning}
All fine-tuning objectives operate on the same representation: the
encoder embedding $z = f_\theta(x)$ of \S\ref{sec:method-notation},
instantiated as the 768-d CLS token the video backbone produces for a
32-frame clip $x$, which is also exactly the feature later re-extracted for
evaluation. A linear head $h$ maps $z$ to logits over the $C=|\mathcal{G}|$ glosses
of the training vocabulary. Every run minimises a total loss of the form
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:master}
\mathcal{L} \;=\; \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}
\;+\; \lambda_{\mathrm{cons}}\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}
\;+\; \lambda_{\mathrm{sup}}\, \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}^{\mathrm{gloss}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}}$ stands for the run's
appearance-invariance term (the cosine pull below, the CLS-level contrast
that replaces it, or the pair of SDDA terms with their own weights),
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}^{\mathrm{gloss}}$ for the optional gloss-level
contrastive auxiliary, and the runs differ only in which weights are
non-zero and in what the invariance term treats as positives. The
paragraphs that follow define these ingredients; they belong to the
backbone fine-tuning runs only, not to the video--text head.

\paragraph{Cross-entropy (all backbone fine-tuning runs).}
Standard softmax cross-entropy $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}} =
-\log p_{h(z)}(y)$ against the gloss label $y$. In paired runs the
augmented copies are classified too, carrying their source video's label
(augmentation-as-data), so CE is averaged over the $B$ originals and $M$
augmentations jointly.

\paragraph{Appearance-consistency pull (invariance option 1).}
For each original $z_i$ and each of its appearance-augmented copies
$z_i^{a} = f_\theta(T_a(x_i))$, $a \in \mathcal{A}(x_i)$, present in the batch, a
positive-only cosine pull with no negatives:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cons}} \;=\;
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{(i,a)\in\mathcal{P}}
\bigl(1 - \cos(z_i,\, z_i^{a})\bigr),
\end{equation}
weighted by $\lambda_{\mathrm{cons}}=0.5$. This directly asks the encoder
to make $z$ invariant to the edited appearance attribute.

\paragraph{CLS-token supervised contrast (invariance option 2).}
A stronger variant that replaces the pull by adapting the SupCon
objective~\cite{khosla2020supervised}: we keep its loss form over
$\ell_2$-normalised tokens but redefine the positives so that an
original and all its augmentations, the counterfactual set
$\mathcal{T}(x)$, share one \emph{instance} label (the positive set
$P(u)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:positive-set}), and every other clip in the
batch is a negative,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:supcon}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}} \;=\;
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u} \frac{-1}{|P(u)|} \sum_{p \in P(u)}
\log \frac{\exp(z_u \cdot z_p / \tau)}
{\sum_{v \neq u} \exp(z_u \cdot z_v / \tau)},
\end{equation}
with temperature $\tau = 0.07$, where $u$ and $v$ range over the $N$ clips
in the batch (anchors with an empty positive set are excluded from the
average). Here this term takes the pull's place in the total loss, with the
same weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{cons}} = 0.5$. Unlike the consistency pull, this also
repels different videos, preventing representation collapse.

\paragraph{Gloss-level supervised contrast (optional auxiliary).}
The same objective as Eq.~\eqref{eq:supcon}, but positives are defined by
the gloss label $y$ instead of the source instance: all same-gloss clips in
the batch are pulled together across signers. Off by default
($\lambda_{\mathrm{sup}}=0$; the run that enables it uses
$\lambda_{\mathrm{sup}}=1$).

\paragraph{SDDA-style invariance (invariance option 3, adversarial).}
Following Fu et al.~\cite{fuSignerDiversitydrivenData2024b}, this variant
replaces the cosine pull with two terms: a KL-consistency loss between the
gloss posteriors of an original and its augmented copy,
$\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p_{h(z_i)} \,\|\, p_{h(z_i^a)}\bigr)$ (weight $1$), which
enforces prediction-level rather than embedding-level agreement, and an
adversarial original-vs-augmented discriminator trained on $z$ through a
gradient-reversal layer (weight $10^{-4}$), so that the encoder is
penalised for representations from which the discriminator can still tell
originals from appearance-edited copies.

\subsubsection{View-contrastive encoder}
Architecturally, the view-contrastive encoder is the \emph{video tower}
of the same contrastive model used for the head experiments
(Table~\ref{tab:objective_map}), consuming frozen backbone features
through the identical input projection, but trained from the standard
language-model initialisation rather than from any text-aligned
checkpoint, and with the text tower never called: the continuous corpus
has no vocabulary to align against. It is trained
with the same SupCon form as
Eq.~\eqref{eq:supcon}, with positives defined by the sign-recording
identity (the continuous-corpus label space $\mathcal{Y}$ of
\S\ref{sec:method-notation}): all $K$ views of a sign (real cameras, avatar renders, or
generative edits, depending on the arm) share one label, and views of
other signs are negatives.
At $K{=}1$ the positive set is empty and
Eq.~\eqref{eq:supcon} yields no gradient, so the single-view baseline arm
instead trains with a video-only InfoNCE: each clip in the batch is treated
as its own class, and a cross-entropy is taken over the pairwise cosine
similarities of the $\ell_2$-normalised embeddings, scaled by the same
$\tau = 0.07$.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:setup-datasets}
\textbf{NGT744} is an unreleased Dutch Sign Language (NGT) corpus
recorded by members of SignLab Amsterdam, and the corpus of our
low-diversity regime. The \emph{train} set consists of 744 unique
sentences performed by 4 signers in mocap suits; repeated takes yield
1{,}313 sentence performances, each recorded simultaneously from three
camera angles (VisLab studio, using the motion-capture setup described
in \citet{NGT_motion_capture}). The
\emph{test} pool holds reference recordings of the same sentences (Bushuis studio, one camera angle), originally collected
for the ZINinNGT learning app~\cite{ritmeester2024zininngt} and likewise
not released as a standalone corpus. From this pool we removed every
recording by the four training signers, leaving an evaluation set of 364
sentences, each with exactly one reference video by one of six signers
unseen during training. Evaluation on this corpus is therefore low-data,
cross-signer, and cross-studio.

The NGT744 experiments use this corpus under two splits, summarised
in Table~\ref{tab:ngt_setups}. The synthetic-view experiment
(Table~\ref{tab:ngt_synthetic_views}) and the signer-count
ablation (Table~\ref{tab:ngt_signer_count}) draw on all four
signers and are scored on the same frozen 364-sentence evaluation set,
used for every arm and condition, so train and test counts need not
match. 
The signer-count ablation additionally fixes data
volume: every condition trains on exactly 180 sentences (one deterministic
take each, hence 180 front-view videos and an identical update budget),
split evenly across its $S$ nested signers, with both arms of a condition
sharing identical sentences and takes, so only real-signer diversity
varies.

\section{Models and training settings}
\label{sec:setup-training}
We train three models using the synthetic counterfactuals of
\S\ref{sec:setup-generation}: a fine-tuned Logos backbone and a
SignCLIP-style video--text head on ASL Citizen, and a view-contrastive
encoder on NGT744. Two heads recur throughout: the SignCLIP-style
video--text head described below, and a plain linear head, used both as
the gloss classifier during backbone fine-tuning and as the probing
instrument of the signer-decoding analyses.

\subsection{Backbone fine-tuning (ASL Citizen)}
Our primary \emph{backbone} (the pretrained video network that heads
are trained on top of and that the fine-tuning experiments update) is
\textbf{Logos}~\cite{ovodovLogosWellTemperedPretrain2025}, an MViTv2-S
video transformer~\cite{liMViTv2ImprovedMultiscale2022} pretrained on
Logos+AUTSL+WLASL. We fine-tune it with a gloss cross-entropy objective,
optionally combined with an appearance/signer-invariance term operating
on each video together with its augmented copies: cosine-consistency
pull, CLS-token supervised contrast, SDDA-style KL-consistency plus
gradient-reversal adversarial
discriminator~\cite{fuSignerDiversitydrivenData2024b}, or gloss-level
supervised contrast across signers. Frozen 768-d CLS features are then
re-extracted and evaluated with the benchmark's dictionary-retrieval
protocol and a linear probe (\S\ref{sec:eval-protocols}), both on the
identical signer-disjoint construction.

Independently of the loss, we vary how much of the 16-block backbone
adapts: the top $k \in \{2, 6, 16\}$ blocks are unfrozen (head always
trainable), with layer-wise learning-rate decay
$\mathrm{lr}(d) = \mathrm{lr}_{\mathrm{base}} \cdot \eta^{\,d_{\max}-d}$
once more than the top two blocks train ($\eta = 0.75$ at $k{=}6$,
$\eta = 0.9$ at $k{=}16$; uniform learning rate at $k{=}2$). A two-stage
variant resumes a
converged CE-only checkpoint (backbone and head) and continues at
$100\times$ lower learning rate with the consistency term switched on.

Independent of our fine-tuning choices, one design choice of the Logos
pretraining recipe matters throughout this thesis, because it is baked
into every feature the backbone produces. The organising construct of
the Logos dataset is the
\emph{visually similar sign} (VSSign): signs that share the same manual
form but differ in meaning, covering both polysemy and minimal pairs.
Rather than forcing the encoder to separate such signs, the Logos authors
group VSSigns under one label and show that this yields a \emph{better}
transfer encoder than keeping them apart~\cite{ovodovLogosWellTemperedPretrain2025}.
The consequence for us is that a backbone trained this way is explicitly
rewarded for placing look-alike signs close together: form-organised
structure in its feature space is trained in, not incidental.

Logos is also the thesis's workhorse feature extractor: its frozen
features, form-organised structure included, feed the video--text
heads of the next subsection, the signer probes, and the NGT744 encoder.

\subsection{SignCLIP-head training (ASL Citizen)}
A video--text head is trained from scratch or fine-tuned on top of
frozen features. The head is the SignCLIP dual-encoder
architecture~\cite{jiangSignCLIPConnectingText2024}(built on
VideoCLIP~\cite{xuVideoCLIPContrastivePretraining2021}, itself the
video descendant of CLIP~\cite{radfordLearningTransferableVisual2021}), 12-layer BERT
video encoder, 12-layer BERT text encoder, video--text InfoNCE,
adapted to take video features other than its original MediaPipe pose
input by swapping the first linear layer of its video-token projection
MLP. Original SignCLIP is pose-based; its authors evaluated an RGB
alternative (I3D) only on the simpler fingerspelling task before
committing to pose input, so this adaptation lets us run the
RGB-versus-pose comparison they left open, within one architecture, on
full-vocabulary isolated recognition. Synthetic counterfactuals enter
either purely as additional training data or, in some configurations,
matched to their source video as positive pairs, a
counterfactual-contrastive strategy in the same spirit as recent work
using causal image synthesis to build robust positive
pairs~\cite{roschewitzCounterfactualContrastiveLearning2025}.
We train this head on three families of input features:
(i) \textbf{Logos}, the frozen original backbone and, in the cross
experiments, the fine-tuned variants (CE-FT, aug-FT, SDDA-FT) produced
by the backbone experiments above; (ii)
\textbf{I3D}~\cite{carreiraQuoVadisAction2017}, a 3D-CNN fine-tuned on
WLASL and always frozen here (the older-generation RGB reference
point, retained because it is the RGB baseline in SignCLIP's own
comparison, while no longer the strongest available RGB backbone); and
(iii) \textbf{MediaPipe} Holistic pose
keypoints~\cite{lugaresiMediaPipeFrameworkBuilding2019}, on which we do
not train ourselves: the pose-input rows of our retrieval tables are the
published results of the original SignCLIP model, and our own use of
pose is analytic; the raw keypoint features (a 609-dimensional
mean-pooled or 1218-dimensional mean+std-pooled per-video vector; for
ASL Citizen root-centred at the shoulder midpoint and scale-normalised
to shoulder width per frame are evaluated directly in the
signer probes of \S\ref{sec:results-diagnosis}, where the original
SignCLIP model's embedding space is probed alongside them.

\subsection{NGT744 view-contrastive training}
The same SignCLIP-style
video encoder (12 BERT layers over frozen 768-d Logos features) is
trained \emph{without a text tower}: NGT744 has no gloss vocabulary, so the
unit of supervision is the sign-recording identity itself. Each training
item bundles the $K$ views of one sign (the real front camera plus,
depending on the arm, real side cameras, motion-locked re-renders, or
generative appearance edits), and a supervised contrastive loss pulls all $K$
views of a sign together while pushing other signs apart (video-only
InfoNCE at $K{=}1$, where no positive pairs exist).
Using simultaneous views of one performance as positives is established
practice in action recognition~\cite{shahMultiViewActionRecognition2023}.
The trained encoder
is evaluated by zero-shot cosine retrieval across the real signer gap
(\S\ref{sec:eval-protocols}); the synthetic-view experiment trains on 744
sentence-recordings and the signer-count ablation on 180 per condition,
$K$ views each. Loss details are given in \S\ref{sec:method-losses}.

The synthetic-view arms and the signer-count ablations hold the
\emph{contrastive batch} constant at 64 items for every arm
(equal in-batch negatives at every $K$, with encoder compute scaling
with $K$), and set the learning-rate schedule length to each
dataset tier's actual update count, so that no arm's schedule is cut
short or left mostly in warmup.

\subsection{Generative appearance editing}
We generate appearance counterfactuals with
FLUX.2-klein-9B~\cite{blackforestlabsFLUX2Klein2026}, an open
rectified-flow transformer image editor, run on a single 48\,GB L40S GPU
(or, when available, a shared H100 partition). On ASL Citizen we augment a
targeted subset of the training data, $6{,}000$ source videos ($14.9\%$ of the 40k-video
training set) yielding $24{,}000$ augmented clips, rather than the full
corpus. The primary reason is the generation compute budget:
frame-by-frame editing of all 40k source videos was beyond the GPU
allocation available to this thesis, so a subset had to be selected.
Given that constraint, evidence that augmenting a targeted portion of the
data can outperform augmenting
everything~\cite{nguyenDoWeNeedSynthetic2025} supports choosing the subset
deliberately rather than at random, using the worst-first selection
strategy of \S\ref{sec:method-diffusion}. The selected set covers the
$450$ glosses with the fewest training signers ($8$--$13$, median $12$,
against a corpus median of $14$) and below-average baseline separability
(mean proxy accuracy $0.81$ vs.\ $0.88$ corpus-wide). Two properties of this
selection matter later. It concentrates the augmentation where real signer
diversity is thinnest and invariance is hardest to learn from real data
alone, though ASL Citizen has no truly signer-starved classes (minimum
$8$ signers), which bounds the headroom any signer-diversity intervention
can have in this corpus. And it makes the augmented pairs a deliberately
biased, hard-class sample, which is why the feature-geometry analysis
(\S\ref{sec:results-aug-geometry}) pairs it with a random held-out control. A further signer-stratified
random sample of $500$ \emph{test-split} videos is augmented with the
identical pipeline, solely as the held-out evaluation set for the
feature-geometry analysis (\S\ref{sec:results-aug-geometry}); it is never
used in training.

\section{Evaluation protocols}
\label{sec:eval-protocols}
For ASL Citizen we use three evaluation modes. The first is
\emph{dictionary retrieval}, the benchmark's own
protocol~\cite{aslcitizen}: queries are test-split videos, the gallery
is the train split, and glosses are ranked by their nearest gallery
video in cosine distance, reported as DCG and R@1/R@5/R@10. Our
implementation is a direct port of the benchmark's official reference
code, and SignRep evaluates frozen features under the same
protocol~\cite{wongSignRepEnhancingSelfSupervised2025}. The second is
\emph{video-to-text retrieval} for the video--text heads: the same
dictionary idea transplanted into the head's joint space, with one text
prototype per gloss serving as the dictionary entry. The third is a
\emph{linear probe} on frozen features (gloss classification with a
linear head), the standard frozen-representation readout in
representation learning. The probe is not part of the benchmark
protocol, and we add it deliberately: retrieval scores the metric
geometry of a space while the probe scores its linearly accessible
information, and the two dissociate repeatedly in our results
(\S\ref{sec:results-ft-probes}). It is also the gloss-side twin of the
signer-decoding probe, so together the pair reads as what a space keeps
of the task versus of signer identity. All modes are scored on an
identical signer-disjoint construction across all configurations within
any table.\todome{protocols rewrite 04.08: per-mode scoping after

everywhere, they exist in no external work}

For NGT744 we report cross-signer retrieval
(R@1/R@5/R@10, MRR) between a held-out real signer and reference-signer
embeddings, in both directions. 

\paragraph{Paired significance testing.} When two arms are trained on the
same data and evaluated on the same queries, we test whether they actually
differ using McNemar's exact test~\cite{mcnemar1947note} on top-1
correctness, a standard choice for comparing two models on a shared test
set~\cite{dietterich1998approximate}. Since both arms answer the same
queries, every query lands in one of four cells: both right, both wrong,
only the baseline right, or only the treatment arm right. The first two
cells just reflect how easy the query is and say nothing about the
difference between the arms, so the test uses only the disagreements. If
the two arms were equally good, each disagreement would go either way with
probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$, so the number of disagreements won by the
treatment arm follows a fair-coin binomial, and we compute an exact
two-sided $p$-value from it. The test is standard; as our reporting
convention we propose running it independently per seed and reporting
how many of the 3 seeds reach $p<0.05$. With only 3 seeds, error bars on
the mean gain are wide, but each seed contributes 50--100 disagreeing
queries to the paired test, which makes small gains testable (e.g.\ the
multiview panel of Table~\ref{tab:ngt_signer_count}).
