\section[Distributional structure without language supervision]{Distributional structure without language supervision:
gendered-pair analogies and iconicity}
\label{sec:results-analogy}
SignCLIP illustrates its language-aligned space with the classic
$\mathrm{KING}-\mathrm{MAN}+\mathrm{WOMAN}\approx\mathrm{QUEEN}$ analogy. We
ask whether such relational structure requires text alignment at all, or
already emerges in a purely visual, gloss-supervised space. Let $c_g$ be the
$\ell_2$-normalised centroid of gloss $g$ and
$\hat{z}=c_{\mathrm{KING}}-c_{\mathrm{MAN}}+c_{\mathrm{WOMAN}}$. We report
(i) the rank of $\mathrm{QUEEN}$ by $\cos(\hat{z},\cdot)$ over all $2{,}731$
gloss centroids (operands excluded), and (ii) the offset gain
$\Delta_{\cos}=\cos(\hat{z},c_{\mathrm{QUEEN}})-\cos(c_{\mathrm{KING}},c_{\mathrm{QUEEN}})$:
whether adding the gender offset moves \emph{closer} to QUEEN than KING
already was. The gain is the crucial control: KING and QUEEN are visually
similar signs, so proximity alone is ambiguous between visual form
(predicting $\Delta_{\cos}\!\approx\!0$) and relational semantics
(predicting $\Delta_{\cos}\!>\!0$, largest for visually \emph{dissimilar}
pairs). We propose a transfer battery: it transfers the offset
$\delta=c_{\mathrm{WOMAN}}-c_{\mathrm{MAN}}$ across gendered pairs
(KING/QUEEN, BOY/GIRL, MAN/WOMAN variants; PRINCE/PRINCESS where present)
and measures its cross-pair consistency, repeated per space (raw frozen
features and each SignCLIP head), so any growth of $\Delta_{\cos}$ with
text alignment is directly visible. As a complementary lens on
form--meaning coupling, we intersect ASL-LEX iconicity
ratings~\cite{sehyrASLLEX2021} with the ASL Citizen vocabulary and test
whether highly iconic signs cluster by visual form more than matched
low-iconicity signs (\S\ref{sec:results-iconicity}); iconic signs are
where a visual space and a semantic space should agree most.
The enlarged battery (rerun 2026-07-19 after the spelling fix) covers all
four gendered pairs in both transfer directions ($12$ transfer
directions, offset consistency over all $\binom{4}{2}=6$ offset pairs)
across six spaces: raw frozen Logos, three SignCLIP heads (from-scratch,
fine-tuned, fine-tuned+aug), and two pair-trained SupCon heads ($K{=}3$,
$K{=}5$).

\section{Form--meaning coupling: iconicity as a boundary condition
on visual semantics}
\label{sec:results-iconicity}
The analogy battery above compared \emph{pairs} of signs by how similar
they look; iconicity is a property of a \emph{single} sign: how much its
form resembles its meaning. ASL-LEX 2.0 rates iconicity for 2{,}723 signs
on a 1--7 scale, separately for deaf signers and hearing
non-signers~\cite{sehyrASLLEX2021,caselliASLLEX2017}. At the top of the
deaf-signer scale are signs that depict their referent: EYEGLASSES
(6.77), TREE (6.32), SCISSORS (6.03), MONKEY (5.94), BOOK (5.63). At
the bottom are signs whose form is conventional: NEPHEW (1.14), DOG1
(1.71), COMPUTER (1.84), NIGHT1 (2.19); all nine are in the ASL Citizen
vocabulary. We use the deaf-signer ratings (D.Iconicity) as primary: the
two groups can disagree substantially (TREE is 6.32 for deaf raters but
4.23 for hearing ones), and our question concerns signers' own
form--meaning mappings; the hearing ratings serve as a robustness check.

Iconicity matters for our evaluation for a simple reason. For a highly
iconic sign, looking alike and meaning alike are the same thing, so even
a purely visual embedding space will appear semantic on iconic
vocabulary: whatever clusters HAMMER with hammering-like motion needs
no concept of hammering. Only on arbitrary signs, where form and meaning
come apart, can we tell whether a space encodes meaning or just
appearance. The KING/QUEEN versus BOY/GIRL contrast above was a
hand-picked, two-pair version of this argument; ASL-LEX makes it
vocabulary-wide and graded. It also cuts the other way: reports of
``semantically meaningful'' neighbourhoods in sign-video embedding
spaces are hard to interpret unless iconicity is controlled.

Before testing iconicity itself, we measure how strongly each space is
organised by phonological \emph{form}. We propose a \emph{form-dose}
measure for this: for every pair of gloss
centroids we count how many of five ASL-LEX~2.0 form
features~\cite{sehyrASLLEX2021} the two
glosses share (Movement, MajorLocation, MinorLocation, Handshape,
SignType; 0--5 shared), and bucket the pairwise cosine similarities by
that count (\texttt{form\_dose.py}). Figure~\ref{fig:form_dose}a shows
the result for the frozen space: similarity rises steadily with shared
form, from a mean of $0.01$ at zero shared features to $0.18$ at five.
Panel~b overlays the same dose--response for all thirteen spaces (each
curve shifted so its own 0-shared level is zero), and panel~c reduces
each space to one number: the Spearman correlation between
shared-feature count and cosine over all centroid pairs. The
cross-model picture is consistent: backbone fine-tuning \emph{flattens}
the form organisation ($\rho$ drops from $.06$ to $.00$--$.03$), the
SignCLIP heads keep it near the frozen level, SignCLIP heads trained on
fine-tuned backbones inherit the flattening, and the pair-SupCon heads
\emph{amplify} it ($\rho = .09$ at $K{=}3$, $.16$ at $K{=}5$):
training with appearance counterfactuals as positives makes the space
more form-organised, not less.

The iconicity test compares the top against the bottom iconicity
tertile, per space. The vocabulary join is near-total: the ASL Citizen
split files carry an ASL-LEX code per gloss, 2{,}725 of the 2{,}731
train glosses hold a valid code (six are unannotated), and 994 have a
deaf-signer iconicity rating, giving tertiles of 331 glosses. Because
iconicity covaries with other lexical properties, each high-tertile
gloss is matched to a low-tertile gloss of the same lexical class with
the nearest subjective frequency (tie-break: nearest phonological
neighbourhood density), leaving 248 matched pairs. For each pair we
compare six per-gloss measures: compactness of the gloss's videos
around their centroid; margin to the ten nearest other-gloss centroids;
video$\to$centroid retrieval accuracy; and the fraction of the ten
nearest centroids that share the gloss's major location, handshape, or
movement. Each contrast gets a paired sign-flip permutation test with
$10^4$ resamples.

Figure~\ref{fig:iconicity_contrast} shows every contrast in every
space. Our prediction was that iconicity would be invisible in the raw
space and that a high$-$low contrast would \emph{emerge} with text
alignment; the data say roughly the opposite. In the frozen space,
high-iconicity signs sit in less location-typical neighbourhoods
($-0.080$, $p=0.004$) and are slightly less compact ($-0.013$,
$p=0.030$) than their matched low-iconicity partners. In the SignCLIP
heads these contrasts shrink and lose significance. The pair-trained
heads bring them back: $K{=}5$ restores the location contrast
($-0.097$, $p=0.002$) and both pair heads show the compactness deficit
($p\approx0.03$). Two cautions apply. First, the effects are small,
and across 36 tests one or two cells at $p\approx0.03$ are expected by
chance; the two location-overlap cells at $p<0.005$ are the only ones
we would defend individually. Second, the hearing-rating robustness
pass shows essentially nothing (one cell at $p=0.047$ across 36
tests), so whatever the frozen space picks up is specific to
deaf-signer iconicity. A reading consistent with this pattern is that
iconic signs take their form from what they depict rather than from
the common phonological inventory, so they have fewer form-neighbours
to begin with; a visual space mirrors that lexicon-level fact, text
alignment re-ranks neighbourhoods and blurs it, and form-amplifying
pair training sharpens it again. We flag this as an interpretation
consistent with the data, not a demonstrated mechanism.
